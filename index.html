1st Workshop on Scientific Superintelligence

ICLR 2027 Workshop Proposal

How can AI discovery systems generate new, independently verifiable scientific knowledge and improve their ability to discover it?

April 2027 · San Francisco, California (exact date and room to be announced)

About

Scientific AI is moving from predicting properties and answering questions toward systems that formulate hypotheses, construct models and instruments, design experiments, and learn from their results. The 1st Workshop on Scientific Superintelligence will bring together machine learning researchers and scientific practitioners to examine the algorithms, representations, and evidence needed for this transition. Its central question is: How can AI discovery systems generate new, independently verifiable scientific knowledge and improve their ability to discover it?

We use scientific superintelligence as a research ambition: systems that could surpass expert human teams on clearly specified scientific discovery tasks while producing reliable, transferable, and understandable results. Establishing such capability requires explicit baselines, resource accounting, and external validation; it cannot be inferred from fluent explanations or isolated benchmark scores.

The workshop spans AI for Science (AI4Sci), materials (AI4Mat), chemistry (AI4Chem), mathematics (AI4Math), physics (AI4Physics), AI research itself (AI4AI), and self-driving laboratories. These domains share a discovery loop but differ in what counts as evidence. Four invited talks, contributed presentations, two poster sessions, an evidence clinic, and structured discussions will develop a common evaluation framework, benchmark concepts, and a community research agenda.

Key Questions

  1. Representations for discovery. How should systems encode hypotheses, mechanisms, constraints, uncertainty, and experimental history so that they can reason across modalities and revise a mistaken premise? We will compare symbolic, geometric, graph-based, and learned representations on concrete discovery tasks.
  2. Learning from interventions. How can active learning and reinforcement learning choose experiments that distinguish explanations rather than merely optimize a proxy? The workshop will contrast information gain, objective improvement, experiment cost, and robustness to delayed or noisy feedback.
  3. Verified novelty and capability. What constitutes a discovery rather than retrieval, recombination, or benchmark leakage? Participants will specify temporal splits, expert-team and algorithmic baselines, independent checks, and accounting for compute, laboratory access, human intervention, and unsuccessful trials. Formal proof, predictive simulation, and experimental replication will be treated as distinct evidence types.
  4. Recursive tool and model improvement. Can agents construct better simulators, instruments, abstractions, or evaluators, then demonstrate better discovery with them? Proposed improvements must be tested against fixed external criteria so that modifying an evaluator cannot manufacture apparent progress.
  5. Understanding and responsible autonomy. How can machine-scale exploration yield compact mechanisms humans can test and use? We will examine provenance, calibrated uncertainty, human oversight, safe experiment boundaries, and access for researchers with limited compute or laboratory resources.

Call for Papers

This call is provisional and takes effect if ICLR 2027 accepts the workshop (decisions expected November 29, 2026).

We invite submissions on how AI discovery systems can generate new, independently verifiable scientific knowledge and improve their ability to discover it. The call will prioritize ongoing or novel research, including methods, theory, new experiments, replication, negative results, and well-supported perspectives. We welcome submissions from representation learning, generative modeling, RL, and agent researchers; materials scientists, chemists, mathematicians, and physicists; laboratory automation practitioners; and researchers studying scientific evaluation and responsible AI.

Scope

One discovery loop, multiple forms of evidence

Represent, then Propose, then Test, then Verify, then Revise, and repeat.

Represent→ Propose→ Test→ Verify→ Revise↺

The feedback loop can update a hypothesis, model, representation, experimental protocol, or research tool. Scientific progress depends on whether those updates improve knowledge under a credible validation process. We invite the following applications when they address at least one shared methodological question (see Key Questions):

AI for Science (AI4Sci)

  • Scientific foundation models
  • Multimodal evidence
  • Hypothesis generation and cross-domain transfer

Discovery and validation question: What transfers across domains, and what requires domain-specific structure or measurement?

AI for Materials (AI4Mat)

  • Inverse design
  • Synthesis-aware generation
  • Hierarchical materials and structure–property relations

Discovery and validation question: Do proposed designs retain their properties under independent simulation, fabrication, and characterization?

AI for Chemistry (AI4Chem)

  • Reaction and catalyst discovery
  • Molecular design
  • Synthesis planning and mechanism inference

Discovery and validation question: Can a system propose executable protocols and learn from failures, controls, and repeat measurements?

AI for Mathematics (AI4Math)

  • Conjecture generation
  • Proof search
  • Formalization and algorithm discovery

Discovery and validation question: Are claims checked by an appropriate proof system or independently verified computation?

AI for Physics (AI4Physics)

  • Scientific simulators
  • Equation discovery
  • Inverse problems
  • Experimental control

Discovery and validation question: Do results respect physical constraints and survive tests beyond the training regime?

AI for AI Research (AI4AI)

  • Automated ML research
  • Architecture and optimizer discovery
  • Research-tool improvement

Discovery and validation question: Do improvements survive held-out evaluations, ablations, and matched compute budgets?

Self-Driving Labs

  • Active experimental design
  • Robotics
  • Instrument APIs
  • Scheduling and sim-to-real transfer

Discovery and validation question: Can planning, execution, measurement, and recovery form an auditable, reproducible loop?

Submission Guidelines

Format:

All submissions must be a single PDF file. The workshop has two non-archival tracks, submitted through OpenReview. The main text must be self-contained; reviewers are not required to read beyond it.

  • Research Track: Up to eight pages of main text, with references and optional appendices excluded. Appropriate contributions include discovery algorithms, scientific representations, learning theory, evaluation methods, and experimentally grounded systems.
  • Tiny/Short Paper Track: Two to four pages of main text, with references excluded. We welcome a modest but self-contained result, a simple implemented idea, an additional experiment or re-analysis, a failed replication, or a fresh evidence-based perspective. This track shares the February deadline; it does not introduce a later acceptance round.
ICLR 2027 Guidelines

In line with the ICLR 2027 workshop guidelines, our tiny/short paper track prioritizes “less-than-full-conference” contributions and aims to broaden participation, especially from early-career researchers and authors outside the usual ML publication circuit. AI assistance is permitted, but tiny/short papers must be primarily human-authored and cannot be AI-generated. Authors of accepted papers may apply for ICLR 2027 Financial Assistance through a separate application; details will be posted on iclr.cc/Conferences/2027.

Style file:

Both tracks will use the official ICLR 2027 LaTeX template and anonymous review. Include references and supplementary material in the same PDF. Please change the template’s running header, which refers to the main conference, to refer to the workshop (e.g., “Under review at the SSI Workshop at ICLR 2027”). Submissions that alter the ICLR style (e.g., by decreasing margins or font sizes) or exceed the page limits may be rejected without review.

Review process:

Every submission will receive at least two human reviews, with a third review or independent adjudication where needed. Reviewers will assess scientific relevance, soundness, clarity of claims, adequacy of validation, novelty relative to disclosed prior work, and value for discussion. Short papers will be judged against their stated scope.

Evidence expectations:

Wet-lab access and large compute budgets will not be prerequisites: a careful analysis, formal result, or openly reproducible small-scale study can make a strong contribution. For empirical discovery claims we will request appropriate baselines, uncertainty, provenance, resource costs, and the extent of human intervention. Artifacts will be encouraged where release is feasible. We will encourage release of unsuccessful trials and limitations to make selective reporting visible. Work involving experiments should describe relevant oversight and operating constraints.

Prior work and non-archival policy:

Previously published work may provide context, but a submission must clearly identify new content; the workshop will not primarily re-present papers from the ICLR main track or other ML conferences. Acceptance will not constitute an archival proceedings publication. Accepted papers will remain publicly accessible on OpenReview and be listed on the workshop website. Authors must check the policies of any concurrent venue.

Visibility:

Submissions and reviews will not be public. Only accepted papers will be made public on OpenReview.

Double-blind reviewing:

All submissions must be anonymized and may not contain any identifying information that violates the double-blind reviewing policy. This applies to supplementary and linked material as well, including code; if you link to external material, you are responsible for ensuring anonymous browsing. Please do not include acknowledgements at submission time, and cite your own prior work in the third person. Papers that violate this policy may be rejected without review.

Conflicts of interest:

An organizer will not handle a submission from their own institution or organization, including any part of the same corporation. Coauthorship, advising, collaboration, personal, and financial conflicts will also require recusal. A non-conflicted chair will assign reviewers and make the decision. If all organizers are conflicted, an independent external decision chair will handle the paper. Organizer-authored submissions will receive external handling, and organizers will not present talks at this workshop.

AI participation policy:

The workshop studies AI conducting research, including AI-generated hypotheses, code, proof candidates, and experimental plans. Submissions in both tracks require accountable human authors. AI will not serve as a primary author, peer reviewer, or decision maker. Tiny/short papers must be primarily human-authored and cannot be AI-generated; AI assistance is permitted. All authors will disclose substantive AI use and how outputs were checked, following the ICLR 2027 author guidance.

Reviewers must write their own substantive assessments. Any permitted AI assistance in editing reviews must follow the ICLR disclosure requirements, including preservation of the original assessment and model interactions. Confidential submissions may not be entered into external AI services without appropriate permission and confidentiality protections. Research on AI-assisted review can be presented as research, but will not replace the workshop’s human review process.

Presentation:

ICLR 2027 workshops are held in person. All accepted contributions receive a poster slot; six receive orals and ten receive lightning slots. Selections will cover domains, methods, and career stages, with at least two lightning slots reserved for tiny/short papers, subject to suitable submissions. For exceptional visa or other constraints, we will offer a poster proxy and a short prerecorded presentation on the website.

Evidence clinic:

Authors will volunteer a brief claim, supporting evidence, and an unresolved failure mode. An ML researcher and a domain scientist will help the audience design a falsification or replication test. This is a discussion of submitted evidence, not a live laboratory experiment.

Online access:

Accepted papers, optional posters, captioned prerecorded videos, and discussion notes will be available online. An asynchronous question form will support contributions from those unable to attend. We will request permission and conference support to record invited talks; if recording is unavailable, we will publish consenting speakers’ slides and written summaries.
