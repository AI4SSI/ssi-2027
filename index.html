1st Workshop on Scientific Superintelligence

ICLR 2027 Workshop

What would it take for AI to exceed the best human scientists, and how would we know?

April 2027 · San Francisco, California (exact date and room to be announced)

Call for Papers

About

We treat scientific superintelligence (SSI) as an operational target, not a slogan or forecast: AI systems that match and then exceed the best human scientists, given the same tasks, time, and resources, across the research loop (reading the literature, forming hypotheses, designing and running experiments, analyzing results, proving and verifying claims, and communicating findings). Exceeding these scientists means asking better questions, finding what they miss, and justifying results so others can check them. Measuring this, stage by stage and end to end, is part of our agenda.

Why now, and why ICLR? AI systems already search the literature, propose molecules and materials, steer automated labs, and produce machine-checkable proofs, but mostly one step at a time. Claims of AI-driven discovery also outpace our ability to check them: benchmarks saturate or leak into training data, and novelty is hard to establish. Many open bottlenecks are core ICLR problems, such as learning from costly experimental feedback, test-time reasoning, calibrated world models, tool-using agents, evaluation, and oversight; others lie in data, instruments, and the pace of physical experiments.

This proposed one-day, in-person ICLR 2027 workshop brings together machine learning (ML) researchers, domain scientists, builders of self-driving labs, and researchers in evaluation, safety, and science policy, including skeptics of the SSI framing. Invited talks, contributed talks, Tiny Paper spotlights, and posters present new work; a panel discussion and long discussion blocks take up what counts as a discovery and how to verify it; an open-problems session pairs domain scientists with ML researchers. We especially welcome under-represented, under-resourced, and early-career researchers. Papers, posters, and recordings will be posted online.

Key Questions

  1. What evidence should convince us that an AI-produced result is new, correct, and significant, rather than retrieved from training data?
  2. Which learning signals can close the research loop when experimental feedback is costly, noisy, and delayed, and where do experiments and data, not reasoning, limit progress?
  3. What would a fair, resource-matched comparison with the best human scientists look like, and does superhuman performance transfer across fields or stay narrow?
  4. How can we verify AI-generated claims that no single expert can fully check: formal proof, replication, prospective prediction, or something new?
  5. How should credit, peer review, and human oversight change as AI does more of the research, and which dual-use safeguards must precede expert-level autonomy?

Call for Papers

This call is provisional and takes effect if ICLR 2027 accepts the workshop (decisions expected November 29, 2026).

We invite submissions on AI systems that carry out science across the research loop. We value work on how much of the loop such systems close, how they compare with the best human scientists under matched conditions, how to check their claims, and how to deploy them safely. Replications and negative results are welcome. We encourage submissions from machine learning, AI safety, formal methods, lab automation, biology, chemistry, materials science, physics, mathematics, climate science, science policy, and philosophy of science.

Scope

Topics of interest include, but are not limited to:

Representations, Reasoning & World Models

  • Foundation models over text, sequences, structures, spectra, and images: when scale yields cross-field transfer and when specialization wins.
  • Symmetry-aware representations and learned simulators that stay calibrated and flag out-of-distribution queries, where new discoveries tend to lie.
  • Test-time search over hypotheses, synthesis routes, and proof steps, guided by learned value functions and verifiers.
  • Data for discovery: sim-to-real gaps, synthetic data, and machine-readable experimental records, including failed experiments.
  • Allocating fixed budgets across reasoning, simulation, and physical experiments, and identifying where experimental throughput, not compute, limits progress.

AI Scientists & Self-Driving Labs

  • Literature agents that map evidence, contradictions, and untested predictions across fields, with every claim traceable to a source.
  • Problem selection and hypothesis generation, with measures of novelty and significance that go beyond surface similarity to prior work.
  • Learning to experiment: Bayesian optimization, active learning, and reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards and costly, noisy, and delayed lab outcomes.
  • Long-horizon agents that run code, call solvers and instruments, and recover from failed runs and instrument drift over multi-week campaigns.
  • Analysis and reporting: agents whose write-ups state uncertainty and limitations faithfully and can be checked against the underlying data.

Evaluation, Verification & Reproducibility

  • Contamination-resistant and prospective evaluation: post-cutoff findings, held-out experimental data, unsolved problems, and predictions registered before experiments are run.
  • Testing claims of surpassing the best human scientists: baselines matched on task, time, cost, and information, with expert disagreement measured.
  • Verifiable artifacts: autoformalized statements, machine-checked proofs, certificates for derivations and analyses, and provenance for code, data, prompts, and lab logs.
  • Independent replication of claimed AI discoveries, and detection of p-hacking, data leakage, and fabricated citations.

Humans & the Scientific Ecosystem

  • Interfaces and explanations that let scientists steer, critique, and override AI proposals, favoring mechanisms and derivations experts can check.
  • Interpretability as a route to discovery: extracting testable scientific knowledge from what trained models have learned.
  • Human understanding: over-reliance, deskilling, and the narrowing of scientific judgment when AI results outpace what scientists can follow.
  • What counts as a discovery or explanation, and how credit, authorship, and peer review adapt to AI-generated papers at scale.
  • Equitable access to models, compute, data, and lab automation for under-resourced groups.

Safety, Dual Use & Governance

  • Dangerous-capability evaluations of AI agents for biology and chemistry, including uplift studies and red-teaming.
  • Safeguards for self-driving labs: action constraints, human sign-off, monitoring, audit trails, and who decides when to relax them.
  • Reward hacking and specification gaming when agents optimize proxy metrics or simulator scores.
  • Scalable oversight of scientific reasoning that exceeds the expertise of human evaluators.
  • Release policies for scientific models and responsible disclosure of hazardous findings.

Discovery Across the Sciences

  • Mathematics: conjecture generation, counterexample search, and formal theorem proving at the research level.
  • Life sciences: protein design, genomic analysis, and mechanistic hypotheses tested in pre-registered wet-lab experiments.
  • Chemistry and materials science: catalysts, battery materials, polymers, and small molecules carried through synthesis and characterization, not just computed properties.
  • Physics and climate science: equation discovery, PDE surrogates, and climate emulators tested outside their training regimes.
  • Machine learning itself: agents that reproduce papers, run ablations, and test new methods—a cheap, checkable testbed for automated research and its safety risks.

Submission Guidelines

Format:

All submissions must be a single PDF file. We welcome high-quality original work in the following two tracks. References, appendices, and the AI use statement required by the ICLR 2027 policy do not count toward the page limit, but the main text must be self-contained; reviewers are not required to read beyond it.

  • Full Papers: Up to 9 pages of main text for novel methods, theoretical results, empirical studies, systems, benchmarks, or position papers (including critiques of the SSI framing) on any topic in the scope above.
  • Tiny Papers: Up to 4 pages of main text for early-stage ideas, simple implementations, modest theoretical results, follow-up experiments, negative results, or fresh perspectives on existing work.
ICLR 2027 Guidelines

In line with the ICLR 2027 workshop guidelines, our Tiny Papers track prioritizes “less-than-full-conference” contributions and aims to broaden participation, especially from early-career researchers and authors outside the usual ML publication circuit. AI assistance is permitted, but Tiny Papers must be primarily human-authored; AI-generated papers are not allowed. Authors of accepted papers may apply for ICLR 2027 Financial Assistance through a separate application; details will be posted on iclr.cc/Conferences/2027.

Style file:

Please format your submission using the official ICLR 2027 LaTeX template. Include references and supplementary material in the same PDF. Please change the template’s running header, which refers to the main conference, to refer to the workshop (e.g., “Under review at the SSI Workshop at ICLR 2027”). Submissions that alter the ICLR style (e.g., by decreasing margins or font sizes) or exceed the page limits may be rejected without review.

Dual-submission and non-archival policy:

We welcome ongoing and unpublished work. We will also consider papers that are under review elsewhere, provided this does not violate the dual-submission or anonymity policies of those venues. Since the workshop is meant to showcase new work, papers already accepted or published at a major ML venue (including the ICLR 2027 main conference) should not be submitted. The workshop is non-archival and will not have formal proceedings; accepted papers can be subsequently or concurrently submitted to other venues.

Visibility:

Submissions and reviews will not be public. Only accepted papers will be made public on OpenReview.

Double-blind reviewing:

All submissions must be anonymized and may not contain any identifying information that violates the double-blind reviewing policy. This applies to supplementary and linked material as well, including code; if you link to external material, you are responsible for ensuring anonymous browsing. Please do not include acknowledgements at submission time, and cite your own prior work in the third person. Papers that violate this policy may be rejected without review.

Use of AI tools:

SSI@ICLR2027 adopts the ICLR 2027 AI policies for authors and reviewers. Authors are fully responsible for the content of their submissions and must disclose their use of AI tools as required by that policy. In both tracks, AI tools may assist, but submissions must be primarily human-authored and AI systems may not be listed as authors. Papers that study AI scientist systems, or that report results produced by them, are very welcome.

Presentation:

ICLR 2027 workshops are held in person. All accepted papers will be presented as posters, and a selection will be invited for oral or spotlight talks. At least one author of each accepted paper is expected to attend; if no author can travel (e.g., due to visa issues), we will accommodate the paper with a pre-recorded video and a displayed poster.

Oral and Best Paper Awards:

We plan to select several papers for contributed oral talks and a set of Tiny Papers for short spotlight talks. We will present a Best Paper Award, a Best Tiny Paper Award, and a Best Poster Award decided by on-site voting.
